## Big ideas

- **EAR · Speech is written for the ear.** A listener can't re-read. Working memory holds a few items, so spoken structure has to be announced, chunked and repeated in a way that would be redundant on the page; clarity is judged at the listener's end, not the speaker's.
- **BREATH · Voice is shaped breath.** Every audible quality — volume, steadiness, resonance, crisp consonants — starts with controlled exhalation and is finished by the articulators. Most 'weak voice' problems are breath and mouth problems, not talent problems.
- **SILENCE · Silence is a tool, fillers are a leak.** A deliberate pause marks structure, buys thinking time and adds weight; a filler spends the same time and signals uncertainty. The skill is converting the urge to fill into a silent pause.
- **FIRST · Lead with the point.** Listeners decide in the first sentence what you're saying and whether to follow. Answer first, then support; every supporting point either holds up the top line or gets cut.
- **CONCRETE · Concrete before abstract.** Understanding moves from an example the listener can picture toward the general rule; analogies, numbers and stories are the bridge, and checking the listener's model closes the loop.
- **PRESENCE · Delivery carries the message.** Pitch, pace, volume, eye contact and stillness tell the listener how much to trust the words. Variety holds attention; consistency between voice, body and content builds credibility.
- **LOOP · Record, measure, adjust.** Speakers misjudge their own pace, variety and fillers. Recordings, transcripts and counts (wpm, fillers/min, seconds to the point) turn feel into numbers, and short timed reps with one target each are how the numbers move.

## Practices

- **P1 · Observe and measure.** Count and time what was actually said — wpm, fillers per minute, pause lengths, seconds to the main point — from a recording or transcript, before judging it.
- **P2 · Diagnose.** From a described or recorded delivery, name the root problem (breath, rate, structure, audience fit) rather than the symptom, and choose the fix.
- **P3 · Structure the message.** Choose the frame (answer first, PREP, SCQA, STAR, Monroe) that fits the question, build it in seconds, and signpost it for the ear.
- **P4 · Deliver with the voice and body.** Execute breath, rate, pause, pitch, volume, articulation and physical behaviour on purpose, to a target.
- **P5 · Adapt to the listener.** Read the audience and occasion — novice or expert, class or committee, friendly or sceptical — and change vocabulary, depth, examples and length to fit.
- **P6 · Review and give feedback.** Evaluate a speech (your own or someone else's) against a rubric, commend, recommend and challenge, and set the next rep's single target.

## Unit 1 · Breath, voice and diction

Big ideas: BREATH, EAR

### 1.1 Breath support

Book: rosine-2021 §posture, §inhalation, §exhalation, §breath exercises; byu-voice §intercostals, §diaphragm

- ○ **SP-1.1.A** Breathe low and silently between phrases — ribs and abdomen expand, shoulders stay down — while reading a passage aloud, with no audible gasps.
  - EK: Expansion belongs in the lower ribs, abdomen and back; a 'big breath' raises the shoulders and tightens the throat.
  - EK: Outflow is regulated by slow, even abdominal contraction — 'less is more', don't fill to capacity.
  - P4
- ○ **SP-1.1.B** In a timed rep, sustain a steady exhale on [s] for a target count (build 4 → 8 → 16) and then speak a full sentence on one breath without trailing off.
  - EK: Rosine's 4-in / 2-hold / 4-out pattern, extended to 8 and 16 on [s] or [v], trains even outflow.
  - EK: Sentences that fade at the end usually ran out of air, not volume — mark breath points with a slash.
  - P4 · P1

### 1.2 Projection and resonance

Book: rosine-2021 §resonance, §soft palate; lumen-voice §volume; nca-cs §6

- ○ **SP-1.2.A** Set volume to the room: in a timed rep, deliver the same 30-second passage at conversational, small-room (~25 people) and large-room levels without pitch rising or the throat tightening.
  - EK: Small classroom or office: a few steps above conversational volume; larger rooms need projection or a microphone; beginners usually err too quiet.
  - EK: Projection comes from breath pressure and open resonance (pharynx first, then mouth; nose only for m n ŋ), not from pushing the larynx — strain shows up as rising pitch and hoarseness.
  - Needs: SP-1.1.A
  - P4 · P5

### 1.3 Articulation of consonants

Book: rosine-2021 §consonants: manner, §voiced/unvoiced pairs; byu-voice §articulators, §tongue twisters; lumen-voice §articulation; nca-cs §7

- ○ **SP-1.3.A** Classify a consonant by manner (plosive, fricative, nasal, lateral, affricate) and voicing, and name the articulators that make it.
  - EK: Plosives p b t d k g stop then release; fricatives f v s z ʃ ʒ θ ð partly block; nasals m n ŋ route through the nose; l is lateral; tʃ dʒ are affricates.
  - EK: Voiced/unvoiced pairs: b/p, d/t, g/k, z/s, v/f, ʒ/ʃ, dʒ/tʃ.
  - P1
- ○ **SP-1.3.B** In a timed rep, read a dense technical sentence at 150 wpm with every final consonant and unstressed syllable audible on playback.
  - EK: Dropped and merged sounds — 'gonna', 'dinnt', 'hunnerd', 'probly' — are the common faults; low volume makes them worse.
  - EK: Slowing slightly and using lips, tongue tip and teeth fully restores them; mumbling = small jaw and lip movement, slurring = rate outrunning the articulators.
  - Needs: SP-1.3.A
  - P4 · P1

### 1.4 Pronunciation and word choice for the ear

Book: lumen-voice §pronunciation; nca-cs §5, §7

- ○ **SP-1.4.A** Rewrite a written sentence for the ear: shorter clauses, the subject and verb early, acronyms spelled out on first use.
  - EK: Listeners can't re-read; long noun stacks and late verbs overload working memory.
  - EK: NCA competency 5: language clear, vivid, free of unexplained jargon for this audience.
  - EK: Check unfamiliar names and terms against dictionary audio or someone who knows; pick one accepted pronunciation, rehearse it aloud, use it consistently (NCA 7).
  - P3 · P5

## Unit 2 · Pace, pauses and filler words

Big ideas: SILENCE, EAR, LOOP

### 2.1 Speaking rate

Book: lumen-voice §rate; vs-rate §by context, §practice data; riseguide §Pace; nca-cs §6

- ○ **SP-2.1.A** Compute wpm from a transcript and its duration, and classify the result as slow, presentation-range, conversational or fast.
  - EK: wpm = words ÷ minutes; conversation ≈ 150 (NCVS), comfortable presentation ≈ 125–150, audiobooks 150–160.
  - EK: Below ~100 reads as hesitant or unprepared; sustained >180 overwhelms after a minute or two.
  - P1
- ○ **SP-2.1.B** In a timed 60-second rep, hold 140–160 wpm on an answer and 125–150 wpm on an explanation, checked from the transcript.
  - EK: Top-scored practice talks in VirtualSpeech's data had a median of 138 wpm.
  - EK: Fast is the more common fault; nerves push rate up.
  - Needs: SP-2.1.A
  - P4 · P1
- ○ **SP-2.1.C** Vary rate on purpose within one answer — slow for the key claim or a hard step, faster for context — and point to where on playback.
  - EK: Rate is a signal: slowing marks importance or difficulty, speeding adds momentum; one steady rate for minutes reads as monotone.
  - EK: RiseGuide scores both rate and how much it varies.
  - Needs: SP-2.1.B
  - P4

### 2.2 Pauses

Book: lumen-voice §pauses, §fluency hiccups; clark-2002 §delay signalling; prep §pause for effect; riseguide §Pauses

- ○ **SP-2.2.A** Place deliberate 1–2 second pauses after the main point, between sections and before a key number, and mark them with '/' or '//' on a script before delivering it.
  - EK: Intended pauses add emphasis and signal transitions; the speaker feels them as longer than the listener does.
  - EK: Mark breath and pause points on the outline; a pause after a question gives the room time to think.
  - P4 · P3
- ○ **SP-2.2.B** Classify a described pause as a deliberate pause, a fluency hiccup, or a filled pause, and say whether it helped or hurt.
  - EK: Fluency hiccups come from losing your place or thread; a brief silent hiccup often goes unnoticed or reads as deliberate.
  - EK: A filled pause (um, uh) spends the same time and announces the trouble.
  - P2
- ○ **SP-2.2.C** Start every on-the-spot answer with a silent 1–2 second pause instead of a filler or a restatement of the question.
  - EK: Silent pausers are rated surer than filler users (Brennan & Williams 1995, in Clark & Fox Tree 2002).
  - EK: The opening pause is where the structure gets chosen.
  - Needs: SP-2.2.A
  - P4

### 2.3 Filler words

Book: clark-2002 §uh vs um; laske-2024 §0/2/5/12 per minute; qbs-hr §awareness training, §competing response; lumen-voice §vocal fillers; riseguide §Pauses

- ○ **SP-2.3.A** Count fillers per minute in a recording or transcript, separating filler sounds (um, uh, er) from filler words (like, you know, so, basically, right?).
  - EK: Fillers/min = count ÷ minutes; a student logged ~30 'um's in 3 minutes (10/min).
  - EK: Filler sounds hurt perceived effectiveness more than filler words (Laske et al. 2024).
  - P1
- ○ **SP-2.3.B** In a timed 60-second rep on a random prompt, deliver with ≤ 3 filler sounds (target ≤ 1), measured from the transcript.
  - EK: 12 fillers/min clearly lowered listener ratings; ≤5/min was not significantly harmful — 'acceptable' is the floor, not the goal.
  - EK: Um announces a long delay, uh a short one: frequent ums mean the thinking is happening mid-sentence.
  - Needs: SP-2.3.A, SP-2.2.C
  - P4 · P1
- ○ **SP-2.3.C** Apply awareness training and a competing response: name your top two fillers, catch them on playback, then replace each with a closed-mouth silent pause or breath in the next rep.
  - EK: Awareness training (identify, record, count) alone has reduced filled pauses in JABA studies; habit reversal adds a competing response — a silent pause where the filler would go.
  - EK: Fillers cluster at sentence starts and transitions; 'so' and 'and' chaining sentences is the same leak.
  - Needs: SP-2.3.A
  - P6 · P4

## Unit 3 · Vocal variety and presence

Big ideas: PRESENCE, BREATH

### 3.1 Pitch and intonation

Book: lumen-voice §pitch; nca-cs §6; tm-eval §Vocal Variety; tm-paths §Introduction to Vocal Variety and Body Language, §Understanding Vocal Variety; riseguide §Confidence

- ○ **SP-3.1.A** End declarative sentences with a falling pitch, and in a recorded rep make every claim sentence of a 60-second answer fall rather than rise.
  - EK: Rising terminals on statements (upspeak) sound like questions and invite doubt; a slight drop marks completion, serious points and the ending.
  - EK: A lower pitch at the close cues the room that you're done.
  - P4
- ○ **SP-3.1.B** Diagnose a described voice as monotone, sing-song, upspeaking or pressed, and choose the correction.
  - EK: Speakers believe they vary pitch more than listeners hear — recording is the check.
  - EK: Range exercise: slide from your highest to lowest comfortable note and back; use more of that range for key words.
  - P2

### 3.2 Emphasis and volume dynamics

Book: nca-cs §6; tm-eval §Vocal Variety

- ○ **SP-3.2.A** Mark the one key word per sentence in a script and, on playback, stress it with pitch, volume or a pause before it — and change the meaning by moving the stress.
  - EK: 'I didn't say she took it' has seven meanings by stressed word; emphasis tells the listener where the information is.
  - EK: Stressing everything stresses nothing.
  - P4 · P3
- ○ **SP-3.2.B** Pick the stronger of two recorded or described deliveries of the same line on vocal variety, and name what made the difference.
  - EK: NCA 'excellent' vocal variety: exceptionally varied yet conversational, controlled pace, clearly heard, pitch that enhances the message.
  - EK: Variety serves meaning — random variation is as distracting as none.
  - P6 · P2

### 3.3 Physical presence

Book: nca-cs §8; tm-eval §Eye Contact, §Gestures, §Comfort Level; tm-paths §Effective Body Language

- ○ **SP-3.3.A** Hold eye contact for one complete thought per listener (about 3–5 seconds) and move across the room; on video, look at the lens for whole sentences.
  - EK: Eye contact gauges reaction and engages; darting or reading notes breaks it.
  - EK: On a call the lens is the listener's eyes.
  - P4 · P5
- ○ **SP-3.3.B** Stand or sit grounded — feet planted, hands still at rest, gestures only on purpose — and on video count distracting movements (sway, fidget, touch face) per minute.
  - EK: NCA: posture, gesture, movement, facial expression and eye contact consistently reinforce the message; Toastmasters rates distracting or absent gestures 'Emerging'.
  - EK: Stillness reads as confidence; a gesture that matches a word (size, sequence, contrast) reinforces it.
  - P4 · P1

### 3.4 Confidence under nerves

Book: tm-paths §Confidence (core competency); tm-eval §Comfort Level; prep §never apologise; riseguide §Confidence, §Charisma Mastery

- ○ **SP-3.4.A** Use a pre-speaking routine — two slow exhale-longer breaths, a planned first sentence, shoulders down — before class, a meeting turn or an interview answer.
  - EK: Nerves speed rate, raise pitch and shorten breath; a long exhale and a known first line counter all three.
  - EK: Comfort Level is its own Toastmasters criterion: the audience reads ease from voice and body.
  - Needs: SP-1.1.A
  - P4
- ○ **SP-3.4.B** Cut hedges and apologies from an answer — 'sorry, this might be wrong, but', 'I just think', 'kind of' — and in a rep state the claim plainly with its real confidence level once.
  - EK: Never apologise for an impromptu answer; hedging every clause signals less certainty than you have.
  - EK: Calibrated confidence: say 'I'm fairly sure' once, where it applies, not on every sentence.
  - P4 · P3

## Unit 4 · Structure — answer first, signposting, endings

Big ideas: FIRST, EAR

### 4.1 Answer first and the pyramid

Book: minto-scqa §pyramid principle; nca-cs §2; riseguide §Structure

- ○ **SP-4.1.A** In a timed rep, give the answer or recommendation in the first sentence (within ~10 seconds) of a 60-second response to a random question.
  - EK: Pyramid: the top line first, then the reasons that support it; any point that doesn't support the top gets cut.
  - EK: NCA 'excellent' thesis: every listener can state it within the opening few sentences.
  - P3 · P4
- ○ **SP-4.1.B** Pick the stronger of two openings to the same question — answer-first or context-first — and say what the weaker one makes the listener wait for.
  - EK: Context-first ('So, background…') makes the listener hold details with no frame to hang them on.
  - EK: Busy listeners (committees, managers, interviewers) decide in the first sentence whether to keep listening.
  - P6 · P3
- ○ **SP-4.1.C** Group support into two or three parallel reasons and announce the count ('two reasons: cost and risk').
  - EK: Reasons at one level should be the same kind of thing and not overlap; three is the most a listener reliably holds without notes.
  - Needs: SP-4.1.A
  - P3

### 4.2 Frames — PREP and SCQA

Book: prep §PREP, §past–present–future; minto-scqa §SCQA

- ○ **SP-4.2.A** In a timed 60-second rep, deliver a PREP answer: Point, Reason, Example, Point restated — each element identifiable in the transcript.
  - EK: PREP = state the point, give a reason, support with one specific example, restate the point.
  - EK: One concrete example beats three vague ones.
  - P3 · P4
- ○ **SP-4.2.B** Frame a meeting update or proposal as SCQA — Situation, Complication, Question, Answer — in four sentences.
  - EK: Situation = agreed current state; Complication = what changed or went wrong; Question = the one problem to solve; Answer = the recommendation the rest supports.
  - EK: Close by restating the answer and the ask.
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-4.2.C** Choose the right frame for a described prompt — PREP (opinion), SCQA (proposal), STAR (behavioural), past–present–future (trend), problem–solution — and justify it.
  - EK: The frame follows the question type; forcing STAR on an opinion question or PREP on a story wastes the structure.
  - Needs: SP-4.2.A, SP-4.2.B
  - P3 · P2

### 4.3 Signposting and transitions

Book: nca-cs §4; tm-eval §Clarity

- ○ **SP-4.3.A** Preview, signpost and summarise for the ear: announce the parts, mark each move ('first… the second reason… so'), and recap before closing; in a 2-minute rep, a listener can list your parts afterwards.
  - EK: Spoken structure must be audible — headings don't exist out loud.
  - EK: Tell them what you'll say, say it, tell them what you said: redundant on paper, necessary by ear.
  - P3 · P4
- ○ **SP-4.3.B** Diagnose a described rambling answer — buried point, tangent, no transitions, circling back — and name the restructure.
  - EK: Rambling is usually a missing top line: the speaker is searching for the point while talking.
  - EK: The fix is choosing the point in the opening pause, not talking faster.
  - P2

### 4.4 Openings and endings

Book: nca-cs §4; minto-scqa §close with call to action; prep §strong conclusion; riseguide §Structure

- ○ **SP-4.4.A** End on purpose: restate the point or give the ask in one sentence, drop pitch, then stop — no trailing 'so, yeah, that's it'.
  - EK: NCA 'excellent' conclusion reflects the content and leaves a strong message or call to action.
  - EK: The last sentence is the one remembered; trailing off spends it.
  - P3 · P4

## Unit 5 · Impromptu speaking — table topics, Q&A, interviews

Big ideas: FIRST, SILENCE

### 5.1 Table topics and on-the-spot answers

Book: prep §impromptu speech; tm-paths §The Power of Humor in an Impromptu Speech; riseguide §60-second recordings

- ○ **SP-5.1.A** In a timed rep, take a random prompt, pause ≤ 3 seconds, and deliver a 60-second structured answer that lands within ±5 seconds of time.
  - EK: Impromptu = 1–3 minutes' notice or none; the pause is for choosing the point and the frame.
  - EK: Hitting time is a skill: plan point, one example, close — then stop.
  - Needs: SP-4.2.A, SP-2.2.C
  - P3 · P4
- ○ **SP-5.1.B** When you don't know the answer, say what you do know, what you'd need to find out, and when — in under 20 seconds, without fillers or apology.
  - EK: Bluffing costs more credibility than a clear 'I don't know, here's how I'd find out'.
  - EK: A partial answer stated plainly is still an answer.
  - P4 · P5

### 5.2 Handling questions

Book: nca-cs §2, §5; tm-eval §Audience Awareness

- ○ **SP-5.2.A** Answer an audience question with pause → one-sentence answer → one support → check ('does that get at it?'), in under 45 seconds in a rep.
  - EK: Q&A answers should be shorter than the talk's sections; the questioner wanted one thing.
  - EK: Repeat or rephrase the question only when the room didn't hear it — not as a stall.
  - EK: To return to your message, answer first, then bridge ('…and the bigger point is'); bridging without answering reads as evasion.
  - Needs: SP-4.1.A
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-5.2.B** Classify a described question as clarifying, challenging, off-topic or a comment-not-a-question, and choose the response move for each.
  - EK: Challenge: acknowledge the point, give your reason, don't concede what you don't believe; off-topic: brief answer, offer to follow up; comment: thank and bridge.
  - P2 · P5

### 5.3 Interview answers

Book: star-uw §Situation, §Task, §Action, §Result

- ○ **SP-5.3.A** In a timed 90–120-second rep, answer a 'tell me about a time' question in STAR with roughly 20 / 10 / 60 / 10 time split and a quantified result.
  - EK: Situation ~20%, Task ~10%, Action ~60% (what *you* did, even on a team), Result ~10% with numbers.
  - EK: Most weak STAR answers over-spend on Situation.
  - P3 · P4
- ○ **SP-5.3.B** Diagnose a described STAR answer — too much context, 'we' instead of 'I', missing result, no lesson — and fix it.
  - EK: Use 'I' for your actions; state the outcome with a number and, if asked, what you'd do differently.
  - Needs: SP-5.3.A
  - P2 · P6
- ○ **SP-5.3.C** Deliver a 30-second answer to 'tell me about yourself' or 'what do you work on' that leads with the role and one through-line, then two proof points, tailored to the listener.
  - EK: Present → past → why-this: start with what you do now and the thread connecting teacher, chemist and AI-safety work, not a chronological biography.
  - Needs: SP-4.1.A
  - P3 · P5

## Unit 6 · Explaining technical ideas

Big ideas: CONCRETE, EAR, FIRST

### 6.1 Pitching to the listener

Book: nca-cs §1, §5; tm-eval §Audience Awareness

- ○ **SP-6.1.A** Before explaining, state in one sentence what the listener already knows and what they need to be able to do afterwards, and cut anything that doesn't serve it.
  - EK: NCA 'excellent' topic: suited to purpose, time limit and audience, from real audience analysis.
  - EK: The expert's blind spot: once you know a thing it is hard to imagine not knowing it — name the prerequisite explicitly.
  - P5
- ○ **SP-6.1.B** In a timed rep, explain a concept from your field in three sentences to a novice — what it is, a concrete example, why it matters — with no undefined jargon.
  - EK: Three sentences force the top line; every technical term is either defined in plain words or removed.
  - EK: Test: could the listener repeat the gist in their own words?
  - Needs: SP-6.1.A
  - P5 · P3
- ○ **SP-6.1.C** Re-pitch the same explanation at three levels — novice, adjacent expert, specialist — changing vocabulary, depth and examples while the top line stays the same.
  - EK: Depth and jargon change with the audience; the claim doesn't.
  - EK: Explaining a CBRN eval to a policy lawyer vs an ML engineer is the same idea with different handholds.
  - Needs: SP-6.1.B
  - P5

### 6.2 Concrete before abstract

Book: mcneil-fyfe §concreteness fading; rosenshine §models and worked examples

- ○ **SP-6.2.A** Order an explanation concrete → bridge → abstract: a specific case first, then the general rule or symbol.
  - EK: Concreteness fading (McNeil & Fyfe 2012): concrete, then intermediate, then abstract beat either alone on transfer at 1 and 3 weeks (70% vs 38% at ceiling).
  - EK: Lead with the example the listener can picture; name the rule once they've seen it work.
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-6.2.B** Build an analogy for a technical idea, then state explicitly where the analogy breaks.
  - EK: A good analogy maps the structure (what causes what), not surface features.
  - EK: Saying where it breaks prevents the listener from over-extending it.
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-6.2.C** Pick the stronger of two explanations of the same idea — abstract-first vs example-first, or jargon-dense vs plain — and say why.
  - EK: Example-first wins for novices; experts tolerate abstract-first but still benefit from a worked case.
  - Needs: SP-6.2.A
  - P6 · P2

### 6.3 Small steps and checking understanding

Book: rosenshine §small steps, §ask many questions, §check for understanding, §high success rate

- ○ **SP-6.3.A** Break a multi-step explanation into steps of one idea each, and pause after each step for the listener to try or restate it before moving on.
  - EK: Rosenshine: present new material in small steps with practice after each; aim for a high success rate before moving on.
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-6.3.B** Check understanding with a question that requires the listener to use the idea (predict, apply, explain back) instead of 'any questions?' or 'make sense?'.
  - EK: Effective teachers ask many questions and check every student's response, not just volunteers'.
  - EK: 'Make sense?' gets a nod; 'what would happen if we doubled it?' gets evidence.
  - P5 · P4
- ○ **SP-6.3.C** Diagnose a described misunderstanding from a listener's answer and re-explain with a different example, not the same words louder.
  - EK: A wrong answer reveals the listener's model; repair the model, don't repeat the script.
  - Needs: SP-6.3.B
  - P2 · P5

### 6.4 Numbers, precision and visuals out loud

Book: nca-cs §3; tm-eval §Well Supported

- ○ **SP-6.4.A** State a number for the ear: round it, give one comparison ('about a third — one in three'), and say the unit.
  - EK: Listeners can't hold 3.847 × 10⁻⁴; give the order of magnitude and what it means.
  - EK: One well-chosen number beats a list of five.
  - P5 · P3
- ○ **SP-6.4.B** Talk a listener through a figure or board diagram: say what the axes are, where to look, and the one takeaway, before the details.
  - EK: Supporting material should be clearly tied to the thesis and add clarity; a slide the speaker doesn't narrate is noise.
  - P3 · P4

## Unit 7 · Persuasion and storytelling

Big ideas: FIRST, CONCRETE, PRESENCE

### 7.1 Persuasive structure

Book: monroe §Attention, §Need, §Satisfaction, §Visualisation, §Action; tm-paths §Persuasive Speaking, §Persuasive Influence; nca-cs §3; tm-eval §Well Supported

- ○ **SP-7.1.A** Outline a 2-minute pitch in Monroe's Motivated Sequence — attention, need, satisfaction, visualisation, action — one or two sentences each.
  - EK: Need = the problem, who it hurts and how much; Satisfaction = a concrete step-by-step plan; Visualisation = life once solved (and answering objections); Action = one easy, immediate ask.
  - P3
- ○ **SP-7.1.B** Make a recommendation that names the strongest objection and answers it before the listener raises it.
  - EK: Pre-empting the obvious objection builds credibility with a sceptical audience; ignoring it invites it in Q&A.
  - EK: Back the answer with one piece of evidence and its source in a sentence — NCA: support tied to the thesis enhances credibility.
  - Needs: SP-4.1.A
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-7.1.C** End a persuasive turn with one specific, easy ask (who does what, by when), and pick the stronger of two given asks.
  - EK: 'Let's think about it' is not an ask; 'can we pilot it on two evals by Friday?' is.
  - P3 · P6

### 7.2 Storytelling

Book: tm-paths §Connect with Storytelling

- ○ **SP-7.2.A** In a timed 90-second rep, tell a story with a person, a goal, an obstacle, a turn and a point, and say the point in the last sentence.
  - EK: A story needs someone wanting something and something in the way; the point is what changed.
  - EK: Specific details (a time, a place, a line of dialogue) make it vivid; background beyond that is cut.
  - P3 · P4
- ○ **SP-7.2.B** Choose a short story or case to open a lesson or argument and connect it explicitly to the claim it illustrates.
  - EK: A story that isn't tied back is an anecdote; the bridge sentence ('that's why…') makes it evidence.
  - Needs: SP-7.2.A
  - P3 · P5

## Unit 8 · Listening and conversation

Big ideas: EAR, FIRST

### 8.1 Active listening

Book: tm-paths §Active Listening; tm-eval §Audience Awareness

- ○ **SP-8.1.A** Paraphrase what someone said in one sentence and confirm it ('so your concern is X — right?') before responding.
  - EK: Paraphrase checks you heard the point, not just the words; it also slows a heated exchange.
  - EK: Answer the question they asked, not the one you prepared for.
  - P5 · P4
- ○ **SP-8.1.B** Ask a follow-up question that builds on the speaker's last point rather than changing the subject, and classify given questions as open, closed or leading.
  - EK: Open questions ('what led you to…') invite elaboration; closed ones confirm facts; leading ones push an answer.
  - P5 · P2

### 8.2 Speaking in meetings

Book: tm-paths §Interpersonal Communication, §Strategic Leadership

- ○ **SP-8.2.A** Make a meeting contribution in ≤ 30 seconds: connect to what was just said, give your point first, one reason, and the decision or next step you want.
  - EK: Short, point-first turns get heard; long preambles get interrupted.
  - EK: Linking ('building on Maria's point…') shows you listened and frames your turn.
  - Needs: SP-4.1.A
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-8.2.C** Disagree in a meeting with acknowledge → position → reason → proposal, without hedging the position away.
  - EK: Separate the idea from the person; state your view at its real confidence level and propose what would resolve it.
  - Needs: SP-3.4.B
  - P3 · P4

## Unit 9 · Feedback and self-review

Big ideas: LOOP

### 9.1 Recording and transcripts

Book: lumen-voice §record yourself, §filler log; riseguide §learn → record → analyse → adjust → repeat; qbs-hr §awareness training

- ○ **SP-9.1.A** Record a 60-second rep, get a transcript, and log four numbers: wpm, fillers/min, seconds to the main point, and longest silent pause.
  - EK: These four map onto the app scores: Pace, Pauses/fillers, Structure; Confidence needs listening for pitch and volume.
  - EK: Speakers misjudge their own pace, variety and fillers until they hear and count them.
  - Needs: SP-2.1.A, SP-2.3.A
  - P1 · P6
- ○ **SP-9.1.B** Score a recording against the NCA eight competencies (1–3 each, 8–24 total) and name the lowest one as the next target.
  - EK: NCA competencies: topic, thesis, support, organisation, language, vocal variety, pronunciation/grammar/articulation, physical behaviours.
  - P6
- ○ **SP-9.1.C** Set one measurable target per practice block (e.g. ≤ 2 fillers in 60 s, answer in first sentence), run 3–5 reps, and stop when it holds on 3 in a row.
  - EK: One target per rep: trying to fix pace, fillers and structure at once fixes none.
  - EK: Track the number across sessions; the trend, not one take, is the evidence.
  - Needs: SP-9.1.A
  - P6 · P1

### 9.2 Giving and receiving feedback

Book: tm-eval §Commend / Recommend / Challenge, §8100E3 evaluator speech; tm-paths §Evaluation and Feedback

- ○ **SP-9.2.A** Give a 2–3 minute spoken evaluation of someone's speech: one specific commend, one specific recommend with the moment it happened, one challenge.
  - EK: Toastmasters: what they excelled at, what they may want to work on, a stretch goal; feedback must be tactful, constructive and timed.
  - EK: Specific beats general: 'your pause before the 70% landed' not 'good pausing'.
  - EK: Receiving it: thank, ask one clarifying question, turn it into the next rep's target.
  - P6
- ○ **SP-9.2.B** Rate a described delivery on a Toastmasters criterion (Clarity, Vocal Variety, Eye Contact, Gestures, Audience Awareness, Comfort, Interest, Well Supported) on the 1–5 scale and justify the rating.
  - EK: 5 Exemplary · 4 Excels · 3 Accomplished · 2 Emerging · 1 Developing; e.g. gestures 'distracting or limited' = 2.
  - P6 · P2

## Unit 10 · Lecturing and long-form talks (extension)

Big ideas: EAR, CONCRETE, PRESENCE

### 10.1 Lectures and talks

Book: nca-cs §1, §4; rosenshine §review, §small steps; tm-paths §Presentation Mastery

- ○ **SP-10.1.A** Plan a 50-minute lecture as 10–15-minute segments, each with its own point, example and check for understanding, opened by a short review.
  - EK: Begin with a review of prior learning; attention and working memory reset between segments; each segment gets an answer-first top line.
  - Needs: SP-6.3.A
  - P3 · P5
- ○ **SP-10.1.B** Deliver from a keyword outline instead of a script, and on playback show eye contact and conversational rate held throughout.
  - EK: Reading drops eye contact, flattens pitch and speeds rate; a keyword outline keeps you talking to people.
  - EK: Mark 'SLOW DOWN', breath slashes and stressed words on the outline.
  - Needs: SP-2.1.B, SP-3.3.A
  - P4
- ○ **SP-10.1.C** Fit a talk to its time slot: estimate length from word count at your measured wpm, cut to 90% of the slot, and finish within ±30 s in rehearsal.
  - EK: Minutes ≈ words ÷ wpm; at 130 wpm a 10-minute slot holds ~1 300 words — plan ~1 150 to leave room for pauses and questions.
  - Needs: SP-2.1.A
  - P1 · P3

## Unit 11 · Remote, recorded and microphone speaking (extension)

Big ideas: PRESENCE, BREATH

### 11.1 Calls, recordings and microphones

Book: tm-eval §Eye Contact, §Audience Awareness; lumen-voice §microphone

- ○ **SP-11.1.A** Set up for a video call or recording — lens at eye level, light in front, mic 15–30 cm away — and keep energy one notch above in-person to compensate for compression.
  - EK: Video flattens vocal variety and facial expression; slightly more energy and pitch range reads as normal on screen.
  - EK: With a mic, keep a constant distance; don't project into it.
  - P4 · P5
- ○ **SP-11.1.B** Handle a remote Q&A or interview turn with the lag in mind: finish with a clear falling ending and a handoff ('happy to go deeper on any of that') so no one talks over you.
  - EK: Latency of a few hundred ms causes collisions; clear endings and explicit handoffs replace the visual turn-taking cues lost on calls.
  - Needs: SP-3.1.A
  - P4 · P5
