## Big ideas

- **SPQ · Scale, proportion and quantity.** Chemists count particles they can't see by weighing them. The mole links the particle scale to grams, litres and pascals, and every quantitative question is a chain of proportions between those scales.
- **SAP · Structure and properties.** How electrons are arranged in atoms, bonds and molecules — and the forces between particles that result — explains the properties we measure, from boiling points to acid strength.
- **TRA · Transformations.** Reactions rearrange atoms by breaking and forming bonds. They can be classified, balanced and counted; they proceed at rates set by mechanism; and reversible ones settle at an equilibrium that responds predictably to disturbance.
- **ENE · Energy.** Energy is conserved and flows as heat and work. Bond energies set enthalpy changes, entropy and enthalpy together set free energy, and free energy decides favorability, the equilibrium constant and the voltage a cell can deliver.

## Practices

- **P1 · Models and representations.** Describe and use models and representations — particle diagrams, Lewis structures, energy profiles — to explain chemical phenomena.
- **P2 · Question and method.** Pose testable questions, design and critique procedures, and identify what an experiment can and cannot show.
- **P3 · Representing data and phenomena.** Build graphs, diagrams and equations that represent data or a process (balanced and net ionic equations, titration curves, calibration lines).
- **P4 · Model analysis.** Analyze and interpret models and data: read a spectrum, a graph or a diagram, and say what it implies and where it breaks down.
- **P5 · Mathematical routines.** Solve problems with mathematical relations — dimensional analysis, stoichiometry, ICE tables, logs — with units and significant figures kept straight.
- **P6 · Argumentation.** Make a claim, back it with evidence and chemical reasoning, and predict what changes when a condition changes.

### 2.4 Structure of metals and alloys



- ○ **CH-2.4.A** (SAP-3.D) Explain metallic conductivity, malleability and luster with the electron-sea model, and classify an alloy as interstitial or substitutional from atomic radii.

- ○ **CH-2.5.A** (SAP-4.A) Draw the Lewis structure of a molecule or polyatomic ion that obeys the octet rule.

- ○ **CH-2.5.B** (SAP-4.A) Draw Lewis structures for octet exceptions: incomplete octets, expanded octets and odd-electron species.

### 2.6 Resonance and formal charge



- ○ **CH-2.6.A** (SAP-4.B) Calculate formal charges and use them to pick the best of several Lewis structures.
  - EK: FC = valence e⁻ − lone-pair e⁻ − ½ bonding e⁻; prefer FCs near zero, with negatives on more electronegative atoms.

- ○ **CH-2.6.B** (SAP-4.B) Draw all resonance structures of a species and calculate the average bond order.
  - EK: Resonance forms differ only in electron placement; the real structure is the average, e.g. bond order 4/3 in NO₃⁻; higher bond order ⇒ shorter, stronger bond.
  - Needs: CH-2.5.A
  - P1

- ○ **CH-2.7.B** (SAP-4.C) Decide whether a molecule is polar from its shape and bond dipoles.

- ○ **CH-2.7.C** (SAP-4.C) Assign the hybridization of a central atom and count σ and π bonds.
  - EK: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 domains ⇒ sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d²; a single bond is σ, a double σ + π, a triple σ + 2π.

  - P1

### 2.8 Molecular orbital theory for diatomics

- ○ **CH-8.3.B** (SAP-9.C) Calculate the pH of a weak base solution from K_b.
  - EK: [OH⁻] ≈ √(K_b·C); then pOH → pH.
  - Needs: CH-8.3.A
  - P5
- ○ **CH-8.3.C** (SAP-9.C) Find K_a or K_b from a measured pH and the initial concentration.
  - EK: [H₃O⁺] = 10^(−pH) is x in the ICE table; K_a = x²/(C − x).
  - Needs: CH-8.3.A
  - P5
- ○ **CH-8.3.D** (SAP-9.C) Convert between K_a of an acid and K_b of its conjugate base.

  - Needs: CH-8.3.B
  - P5

### 8.4 Acid–base reactions and buffers



- ○ **CH-8.4.A** (SAP-9.D) Calculate the pH after mixing a strong acid with a strong base.

  - Needs: CH-8.2.A, CH-4.5.D
  - P5
- ○ **CH-8.4.B** (SAP-9.D) Identify what remains after mixing a weak acid or base with a strong one, and classify the result as a buffer, a salt solution or excess strong reagent.
  - EK: React to completion first; weak acid + its conjugate base both present ⇒ buffer.
  - Needs: CH-8.4.A, CH-8.3.A
  - P6
- ○ **CH-8.4.C** (SAP-9.D) Predict whether a salt solution is acidic, basic or neutral and calculate its pH.
  - EK: Conjugate bases of weak acids raise pH; conjugates of weak bases (NH₄⁺) lower it; ions of strong acids and bases are neutral.
  - Needs: CH-8.3.D
  - P5 · P6

### 8.5 Acid–base titrations



- ○ **CH-8.5.A** (SAP-9.E) Calculate the pH at the start, before, at and past the equivalence point of a weak acid–strong base (or weak base–strong acid) titration.
  - EK: Start: weak acid; before: buffer; half-equivalence: pH = pK_a; equivalence: conjugate base only; after: excess strong base.

- ○ **CH-8.5.B** (SAP-9.E) Read a titration curve to find the equivalence volume, pK_a and the analyte's strength, and choose a suitable indicator.

- ○ **CH-8.5.C** (SAP-9.E) Interpret the titration curve of a polyprotic acid.

  - P4

### 8.6 Molecular structure of acids and bases



- ○ **CH-8.6.A** (SAP-9.F) Rank acid strength from molecular structure: H–X bond strength and polarity, oxyacid O count, electronegativity of the central atom, and conjugate-base resonance.

### 8.7 pH and pKa



- ○ **CH-8.7.A** (SAP-10.A) Predict the predominant protonation form of an acid at a given pH from its pK_a.
  - EK: pH < pK_a ⇒ protonated form dominates; pH > pK_a ⇒ deprotonated; pH = pK_a ⇒ 1:1.

### 8.8 Properties of buffers



- ○ **CH-8.8.A** (SAP-10.B) Explain how a buffer resists pH change, writing the net ionic reaction with an added strong acid or base.

### 8.9 Henderson–Hasselbalch equation



- ○ **CH-8.9.A** (SAP-10.C) Calculate a buffer's pH with the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.

- ○ **CH-8.9.B** (SAP-10.C) Calculate a buffer's new pH after adding a strong acid or base.

### 8.10 Buffer capacity

## Unit 9 · Thermodynamics and electrochemistry

Big ideas: ENE, TRA

### 9.1 Introduction to entropy



- ○ **CH-9.1.A** (ENE-4.A) Predict the sign of ΔS for a process from changes in phase, moles of gas, mixing, dissolution and temperature.
  - EK: Energy and matter more dispersed ⇒ ΔS > 0: s → l → g, more gas moles, mixing, heating.
  - Needs: CH-3.1.B
  - P6

### 9.2 Absolute entropy and entropy change



- ○ **CH-9.2.A** (ENE-4.B) Calculate ΔS°_rxn from standard molar entropies.
  - EK: ΔS° = Σ n S°(products) − Σ n S°(reactants); S° of an element is not zero (third law: S = 0 only for a perfect crystal at 0 K).
  - Needs: CH-9.1.A, CH-6.8.B
  - P5

### 9.3 Gibbs free energy and thermodynamic favorability



- ○ **CH-9.3.A** (ENE-4.C) Calculate ΔG° from ΔH° and ΔS°, or from standard free energies of formation.
  - EK: ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° (convert ΔS° to kJ); ΔG° = Σ n ΔG_f°(products) − Σ n ΔG_f°(reactants).
  - Needs: CH-9.2.A, CH-6.8.B
  - P5
- ○ **CH-9.3.B** (ENE-4.C) Decide from the signs of ΔH and ΔS at which temperatures a process is favorable, and find the crossover temperature.
  - EK: −ΔH, +ΔS always; +ΔH, −ΔS never; mixed signs switch at T = ΔH/ΔS.
  - Needs: CH-9.3.A
  - P5 · P6

### 9.4 Thermodynamic and kinetic control



- ○ **CH-9.4.A** (ENE-4.D) Explain why a thermodynamically favorable process can be too slow to observe.
  - EK: ΔG < 0 says it can go; a large E_a can keep it from going at a measurable rate (diamond → graphite).
  - Needs: CH-9.3.B, CH-5.6.A
  - P6

### 9.5 Free energy and equilibrium



- ● **CH-9.5.A** (ENE-5.A) Calculate K from ΔG°.
  - EK: K = exp(−ΔG°/RT); ΔG° < 0 ⇔ K > 1.
  - Needs: CH-9.3.A, CH-7.5.A
  - P5 · drills: th-ce-k
- ● **CH-9.5.B** (ENE-5.A) Calculate ΔG° from K.
  - EK: ΔG° = −RT ln K, with R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).
  - Needs: CH-9.5.A
  - P5 · drills: th-ce-dg
- ● **CH-9.5.C** (ENE-5.A) Calculate ΔG under nonstandard conditions and predict the direction of reaction.
  - EK: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = RT ln(Q/K); ΔG < 0 ⇒ forward.
  - Needs: CH-9.5.B, CH-7.3.B
  - P5 · P6 · drills: th-ce-direction

### 9.6 Free energy of dissolution



- ○ **CH-9.6.A** (SPQ-5.D) Explain whether a salt dissolves from the enthalpy (lattice vs hydration) and entropy changes of dissolution.
  - EK: ΔH_soln = lattice breaking (+) + hydration (−); ordering of water around small, highly charged ions can make ΔS_soln negative.
  - Needs: CH-9.3.B, CH-2.2.B, CH-7.11.A
  - P6

### 9.7 Coupled reactions



- ○ **CH-9.7.A** (ENE-5.B) Calculate ΔG° for coupled reactions and explain how a favorable reaction drives an unfavorable one.
  - EK: Add the reactions and their ΔG°'s; a shared intermediate links them (ATP hydrolysis in biology); light or current can also supply the free energy.
  - Needs: CH-9.3.A, CH-6.9.A
  - P5 · P6

### 9.8 Galvanic (voltaic) and electrolytic cells



- ○ **CH-9.8.A** (ENE-6.A) Label a galvanic cell's anode and cathode, electron and ion flow, salt bridge, and write its cell notation.
  - EK: Oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode; electrons flow anode → cathode through the wire; anode ‖ cathode in notation.

- ○ **CH-9.8.B** (ENE-6.A) Distinguish galvanic from electrolytic cells by the sign of ΔG and the electrode charges.

### 9.9 Cell potential and free energy



- ○ **CH-9.9.A** (ENE-6.B) Calculate E°_cell from standard reduction potentials and predict whether a redox reaction is spontaneous.
  - EK: E°_cell = E°_cathode − E°_anode; don't multiply E° by coefficients; E° > 0 ⇒ spontaneous.

- ○ **CH-9.10.A** (ENE-6.C) Predict qualitatively whether changing concentrations raises or lowers the cell potential, and why a battery dies.
